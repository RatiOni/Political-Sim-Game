# UX review — first session, 2026-10-31

First pass over the **experience** rather than the behaviour. The simulation
works; this asks whether a person sitting down in front of it knows who they
are, what they want, and what to press.

## How this was run

The real scenes, booted in a window and driven through a first session with
frames captured at each step (`tools/_uxshot.gd`, `_uxshot2.gd`, `_uxmenu.gd` —
throwaway, removed after the run). A seeded government campaign (424242),
Ardenia, contextual opening role, at 1920×1080 and again at a genuine
1280×720. Computer-use could not drive the real window — it only resolves
Start-menu applications, and the game is a loose executable — so this is
rendered-frame review, not mouse-feel review. Hit-target sizes and hover
states still need a human at the keyboard.

## Checked and cleared

Named here so nobody chases them later:

- **Desks do close.** Escape closes the open desk, and the desk's own CLOSE
  works. An earlier read that Records would not close was an artifact of the
  harness emitting `pressed` on a button the app had already disabled.
- **Nothing is clipped at 1280×720.** A sweep of every visible Control's global
  rect against the window found zero overflow. The earlier apparent clipping
  came from resizing the root window after layout instead of launching at that
  size.
- **The clock runs unattended.** Thirteen months advanced without jamming.

## Serious

### 1. Turn one tells the player nothing

The opening screen carries three empty panels at once: `NO DECISIONS
WAITING.`, `NO MONTHLY REPORT YET.`, `NO DISPATCHES THIS PERIOD.` Nothing
states which office the player holds, which party they lead, how long the term
runs, or what is wrong with the country. The most prominent actionable control
on the screen is a regional `LAUNCH — 10 PC` for a region the player did not
choose and has no reason to care about yet.

A political simulation's first screen has to answer *who am I, what do I want,
what is the first thing I do*. This one answers none of the three.

### 2. The primary verb is not labelled as one

Time advances by pressing a button whose label is `PAUSED`. There is no
`ADVANCE`, no `NEXT`, no play glyph — the control is named after the state it
is in, not the action it performs. Beside it sit five numbered pips and the
word `READY`. A new player has no way to know that the game starts when they
press the word PAUSED.

### 3. At 1280×720 the player loses every vital sign

`scripts/app.gd:142` hides the whole national panel in compact mode:

```gdscript
find_child("NationalPanel").visible = not compact and not _desk_open()
```

Gone with it: stability, approval, political capital, growth, unemployment,
inflation, debt/GDP, revenue, expenditure, balance, and the entire National
Assembly seat strip. `OfficeLabel` — who the player is — goes too. There is no
alternative route to any of it; at that size the dashboard shows a map and one
region.

The header's own comment, eighteen lines down, gets this exactly right for
itself:

> hiding a destination instead would make it unreachable on exactly the
> machines most likely to be running at that size.

The same argument applies to the state of the player's own government. 1280×720
is a resolution `PLAN.md` promises to support.

### 4. A year can pass before the game admits something is wrong

Playing a year without opening the budget desk puts the state onto an emergency
budget. The player is never told. The monthly report says only `PUBLIC APPROVAL
DETERIORATES (-0.2 POINTS).` The failure finally surfaces indirectly, when an
unrelated action is refused:

> NO NEW PROGRAMMES CAN START WHILE THE STATE RUNS ON AN EMERGENCY BUDGET.
> PASS A BUDGET FIRST.

That refusal is the best piece of writing in the interface — it names the
cause, the consequence and the fix in two lines. The problem is that it is the
*first* time the game says any of it. A missed budget should reach the decision
rail as a dated obligation long before it blocks something else.

## Important

### 5. The budget asks for a decision it will not explain

Sliders report millions and a percentage. Nothing anywhere says what cutting
health and welfare does to households, or what starving a ministry does to
delivery — both of which the simulation models in detail. `PLAN.md` requires
"the information shown before commitment"; this is the screen where that is
most clearly missing.

What *is* good and should be kept: `LIKELY PASS · 75 FOR / 25 AGAINST · 51
NEEDED` with the per-party breakdown underneath. That is a real pre-commitment
signal.

### 6. ~~Three commit buttons, no stated order~~ — withdrawn

This was a misreading of the screenshot. `CALL VOTE` and `WITHDRAW` are
disabled until the plan is tabled, and each carries its reason as a tooltip
(`app.gd` `_apply_budget_actions`, driven by the engine's eligibility). The
order is expressed by the controls; nothing to fix.

### 7. Decoration outweighs data on the data screens

On the budget desk the counting-hall illustration takes roughly 55% of the
width while the eight spending envelopes scroll inside a cramped column — only
five are visible at 1920×1080, and `HEALTH AND WELFARE`, at 29% the largest
single envelope, is below the fold. On the records desk the library takes
roughly 65% and the charts are squeezed into a narrow scrolling strip. The art
is genuinely good; it is winning space from the thing the screen exists for.

### 8. The monthly report is arithmetic, not an account

`GOVERNMENT STABILITY DETERIORATES (-0.2 POINTS).` — no cause, no one affected,
no connection to anything the player did. `PLAN.md` asks this digest for "a
causal account of material changes, rather than a pile of unexplained stat
movement". It is currently the pile.

### 9. Changing desks costs two actions

Opening any desk disables all four header destinations, so moving from Budget
to Legislation means closing first. The greyed-out buttons give no reason, so
it reads as breakage rather than as a rule.

## Copy and polish

| Where | Text | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| `scripts/app.gd:310` | "No monthly report yet. Advance a day once connected." | "Once connected" is bridge-era. Nothing connects any more. |
| `scenes/panels/budget_panel.tscn:32` | "DRAG EACH ENVELOPE; THE SIMULATION ACCEPTS ITS AUTHORISED STEPS." | Describes the implementation, not the act. |
| `scenes/panels/region_brief.tscn:63` | "Pressure, jobs and state reach are supplied by the simulation." | Tells the player there is a simulation. |
| footer | "MINISTERIAL WIRE / LIVE SIMULATION / READY" | A status line about the engine, not the republic. |
| `scenes/menu.tscn:42` | "MINISTERIAL WIRE · JUNE 1993" | The campaign starts 1 March 1993. |
| region brief | "VASHTERA" then "Vashtera" | The region's name is printed twice. |

Also:

- **The title takes about five seconds to become legible**, and the wall of
  capitals around it never stops rolling — it settles to 10% alpha but keeps
  changing forever. The docstring says this is intended as "faint texture", but
  perpetual motion beside the game's name reads as unfinished rather than as
  design. A static settle would carry the same look.
- **The menu tagline is clipped** by the bottom window edge at 720p.
- **Three option buttons carry no label or tooltip**: `CountryChoice`,
  `PledgeChoice`, `ShadowChoice`.

## What is working

Worth saying plainly, because it is most of the screen:

- The ASCII landscape, the map, and the interior scenes are striking, coherent
  and unlike anything else in the genre.
- The map communicates at a glance. `UNREST` / `RESTLESS` / `SEPARATIST` chips
  over the pressure fill answer "where is the trouble" instantly, and a year of
  play visibly calms it.
- The pre-vote party breakdown on the budget is exactly the right kind of
  information at exactly the right moment.
- The blocked-programme message is the model the rest of the interface's
  writing should follow.
- Dispatches have real voice — "HARAVAN AND TURAZ: A FEUD HARDENS".

## Suggested order

1. An opening brief: who you are, what the country's problems are, what the
   first decision is. Fixes finding 1 and most of 4.
2. Rename the clock control and give it an advance affordance. Finding 2.
3. Keep vital signs reachable at 1280×720 — a compact strip rather than
   nothing. Finding 3.
4. Route obligations (an unpassed budget above all) into the decision rail with
   a deadline. Finding 4.
5. The copy table — an afternoon, and it removes every mention of "the
   simulation" from the player's view.
6. Rebalance the desks so the data column leads and the art fills what is left.
